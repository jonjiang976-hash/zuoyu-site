Case notes

What buyers actually asked for

No client names and no logos — those belong to our customers, and most of them are private-label brands who would rather not advertise their supply chain. What we can publish is the brief, the constraint, and what changed as a result.

How to read these: each note answers the same three questions. What was the brief? What made it hard? What did the order end up being? If a brief looks like yours, the constraint section is the part worth reading.

Scenario 01 · Small-batch launch

First collection, four styles

First collection, four styles. A brand owner sends four tech packs at the 150-piece minimum. The step that decides the timeline is wash approval, not sewing — so the wash is quoted and sampled as its own line item, and the bulk date starts once the sealed standard is signed.

Scenario 02 · Coordinated set

Jacket and skirt, one wash

Jacket and skirt, one wash. A matched set needs one wash standard across two garments. Because both run through the same laundry on site, the set is developed and approved as a pair instead of being matched after the fact from two different suppliers.

Scenario 03 · Wash reproduction

Reproducing a vintage wash

Reproducing a vintage wash. A buyer sends a worn garment and asks for the same fade. We rebuild it from fabric weight, wash recipe and laser placement, then keep the approved sample as the sealed standard the bulk is checked against.

What we will not put on this page

Client names, client logos, photographs of branded product on a body, and per-unit prices of a specific order. Three reasons this is a policy rather than an oversight: our customers' supply chains are commercially sensitive; unauthorised use of a brand name is a legal exposure for both of us; and a case note that needs a logo to be credible is not a case note.

If you want to speak to a reference customer, ask at quotation stage. We will ask the customer first, and "no" is a perfectly normal answer from a private-label brand.
